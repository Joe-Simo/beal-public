                                                                                                      Abstract
                                                     Generalised Fermat equation (GFE) is the equation of the form ax p + byq = czr , where a, b, c, p, q, r are
                                                 positive integers. If 1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1, GFE is known to have at most finitely many primitive integer
                                                 solutions ( x, y, z). A large body of the literature is devoted to finding such solutions explicitly for various
                                                 six-tuples ( a, b, c, p, q, r ), as well as for infinite families of such six-tuples. This paper surveys the families
                                                 of parameters for which GFE has been solved. Although the proofs are not discussed here, collecting these
                                                 references in one place will make it easier for the readers to find the relevant proof techniques in the original
                                                 papers. Also, this survey will help the readers to avoid duplicate work by solving the already solved cases.

1 Introduction
1.1 Generalized Fermat equation
Around 1637, Pierre de Fermat wrote in the margin of his private copy of Arithmetica that for any integer n ≥ 3
the equation
                                               x n + yn = zn
has no solutions in positive integers. This statement became known as Fermat’s Last Theorem (FLT), and has
been one of the most famous open problems in mathematics for over 350 years. After cancelling the common
factors if needed, it suffices to prove the non-existence of positive integer solutions such that gcd( x, y, z) = 1.
The FLT has been finally proved by Andrew Wiles1 [149] in 1995, and this proof is widely recognized as one
of the most monumental achievements of modern mathematics.
    Even before FLT had been proven, many researchers started to investigate special cases of the more general
equation
                                                 ax p + byq = czr ,                                             (1.1)
where x, y, z are integer variables, while positive integers p, q, r and integers a, b, c are parameters. If abc = 0,
then (1.1) has at most two monomials and is easy to solve, see [77, Section 1.6] and [148, Section 1.16], so
from now on we will assume that abc 6= 0. If (1.1) is solvable in integers ( x, y, z) 6= (0, 0, 0), then, under
a minor condition2 on ( p, q, r), it has infinitely many integer solutions, and all these solutions are easy to
describe, see [78, Section 3.4.2] and [77, Proposition 4.53]. However, the problem of describing primitive
integer solutions to (1.1), that is, ones satisfying gcd( x, y, z) = 1, turns out to be much more interesting and
delicate.
    The triple ( p, q, r) is called the signature of Eq. (1.1). If min{ p, q, r} = 1 then (1.1) is trivial, see [77,
Section 1.5.1] and [148, Section 1.12], so we may assume that min{ p, q, r} ≥ 2. The theory of primitive
solutions to (1.1) crucially depends on whether the quantity 1/p + 1/q + 1/r is greater than 1, equal to 1, or
less than 1. In the first case, we have the following result proved by Beukers [18] in 1998.
Theorem 1.1. Assume that 1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1 and min{ p, q, r} ≥ 2. Then if (1.1) has at least one
primitive solution with xyz 6= 0, then it has infinitely many such solutions. In this case, there exists a finite
number of parametrized solutions of (1.1) with two parameters such that all primitive solutions can be obtained
by specialization of the parameters to integer values. Moreover, there is an algorithm that computes these
parametrized solutions.
     Theorem 1.1 covers the signatures (2, 2, k) for k ≥ 2, (2, 3, 3), (2, 3, 4), (2, 3, 5), and their permutations.
As a nice example of its application, see [66] for the complete description of primitive integer solutions to the
equation x2 + y3 = z5 .
     In the case 1/p + 1/q + 1/r = 1, which consists of permutations of (2, 3, 6), (2, 4, 4) and (3, 3, 3),
Eq. (1.1) reduces to the problem of computing rational points on certain elliptic curves. This problem is open
in general, but there are algorithms that work well for all specific equations with not-too-large coefficients
a, b, c. For example, as early as in 1954, Selmer [126, 127] solved (1.1) with ( p, q, r) = (3, 3, 3) for all a, b, c
satisfying 1 ≤ | abc| ≤ 500. With modern computers and improved algorithms one may proceed much further
if needed.
     The most interesting and challenging case of Eq. (1.1) is 1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1. In this case, Darmon and
Granville [54] proved the following fundamental result.

    The proof of Theorem 1.2 is ineffective and does not provide an algorithm for actually listing the primitive
solutions to (1.1) for a given p, q, r, a, b, c. This problem is the topic of current intensive research. The case
a = b = c = 1, that is, equation
                                                     x p + y q = zr ,                                        (1.2)
is known as the Fermat–Catalan equation, and has been particularly well-studied. A number of survey pa-
pers exist which are devoted to this equation, see, for example, [9, 92], Section 1.2 in [82], and wikipedia
page https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beal_conjecture. We provide a summary5 of solved signa-
tures ( p, q, r) in Table 1.1. There is also some recent work with partial results for other signatures, see
e.g [5, 8, 9, 17, 21, 22, 30, 41, 43, 44, 48, 49, 50, 51, 60, 71, 74, 91, 96, 103, 121, 146]. Famous Fermat–
Catalan conjecture predicts that (1.2) has only finitely many solutions in positive integers ( p, q, r, x, y, z) such
that 1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1 and gcd( x, y, z) = 1. In fact, the only known such solutions are listed in Table 1.2,
and failing to find other solutions despite extensive efforts [109, 130] suggests that this list might be complete.

million-dollar prize for the proof or disproof that (1.2) has no solutions in coprime positive integers ( x, y, z)
when min{ p, q, r} ≥ 3.

    While there are good surveys of Eq. (1.2), a similar survey for the more general Eq. (1.1) is not available.
There are many dozens of papers studying this equation, and each of them cites just a small portion of others. As
a result, some equations of the form (1.1) have been solved multiple times. For example, equation x4 + 3y4 =
z3 has been solved in 1994 by Terai [144], and then again in 2019 by Söderlund [137].
    The aim of this survey is to summarize the solved cases of the generalized Fermat Eq. (1.1) with | abc| > 1.
This will help the readers to avoid duplicate work by solving the already solved cases. To keep the survey
relatively short, we will not discuss proofs. Despite this, we hope that collecting all these references in one
place should make it much easier for the readers to find the proof techniques in the original papers.
    In the rest of the introduction, we discuss some easy reductions, elementary special cases, and state some
conjectures. Sections 2 to 5 discuss for which parameters ( a, b, c, p, q, r) Eq. (1.1) has been solved. The solved
cases are then summarized in the final table (Table 6.1) in Section 6.

1.2 Easy reductions and elementary special cases
We first observe that we may assume that the integers a, b and c in (1.1) are all positive. Indeed, if an equation
Ax p + Byq + Czr = 0 has A, B, C all of the same sign, then it has no solutions other than ( x, y, z) = (0, 0, 0)
if all p, q, r are even, and otherwise we can change the sign of one of A, B, C by a transformation of one of
the variables to their negative. Hence, we may assume that not all A, B, C are of the same sign, and then the
equation can be rearranged to the form (1.1) with positive a, b, c.
     We are interested in finding primitive solutions to (1.1), that is, ones satisfying gcd( x, y, z) = 1. Because
solutions to (1.1) with xyz = 0 are easy to describe, it suffices to find primitive solutions in non-zero inte-
gers. We will call such solutions non-trivial. Further, if a prime s is a common factor of, say, x and y, then
gcd( x, y, z) = 1 implies that gcd(s, z) = 1, but then (1.1) implies that smin{ p,q} is a divisor of c. Continuing
this way, we obtain the following observation.

Proposition 1.4. Assume that c has no divisors of the form smin{ p,q} for prime s, b has no divisors of the form
smin{ p,r } for prime s, while a has no divisors of the form smin{q,r } for prime s. Then ( x, y, z) is a primitive
solution to (1.1) if and only if x, y, z are pairwise coprime.

     From now on, for all equations satisfying the conditions of Proposition 1.4, we will use the terms “primitive
solution” and “solution in pairwise coprime integers” interchangeably.
     We next observe that if one of the integers ( p, q, r), say r, is not prime, and 1 < d < r is any divisor of
r, then change of variables Z = zr/d reduces (1.1) to an equation of the same form with exponents ( p, q, d).
If 1/p + 1/q + 1/d < 1, then this equation has finitely many primitive integer solutions by Theorem 1.2. If
we list all such solutions ( x, y, Z ), we can then check for which of them z = Z d/r is an integer. This implies
that it is sufficient to solve (1.1) only in the case when either p, q, r are all primes, or ( p, q, r) is a special triple
defined below.
Definition 1.5. A triple ( p, q, r) of positive integers is called special if (i) not all p, q, r are primes, (ii) 1/p +
1/q + 1/r < 1, and (iii) if P, Q, R are positive divisors of p, q, r, respectively, such that ( P, Q, R) 6= ( p, q, r),
then 1/P + 1/Q + 1/R ≥ 1.
    Every special triple is a permutation of one of the triples listed in Table 1.3.
    Some equations of the form (1.1) have no non-trivial primitive solutions for elementary reasons. For ex-
ample, modulo 9 analysis shows that if ( x, y, z) is any integer solution to the equation x4 + y4 = 3z3 , then all
x, y, z must be divisible by 3. Hence, this equation has no non-trivial primitive solutions. More generally, we
have the following trivial observation.
Proposition 1.6. If there exists any prime s and positive integer m such that all solutions ( x, y, z) to (1.1)
modulo sm must have all x, y, z divisible by s, then (1.1) has no non-trivial primitive solutions.
    For equations covered by Proposition 1.6, we say that they have no non-trivial primitive solutions by local
obstructions. All such equations will from now be excluded from this survey.
    As mentioned above, we may assume that either p, q, r are all primes, or ( p, q, r) is a permutation of one of
the special triples listed in Table 1.3. In the second case, at least one of the integers p, q, r is composite. If, for
example, r is composite, and 1 < d < r is any divisor of r, then change of variables Z = zr/d reduces (1.1) to
the equation
                                                  ax p + byq = cZ d .                                            (1.3)
Because ( p, q, r) is a special triple, we must have 1/p + 1/q + 1/d ≥ 1. If 1/p + 1/q + 1/d > 1, then, by
Theorem 1.1, either (1.3) has no non-trivial primitive solutions, or all such solutions can be covered by a finite
number of parametrizations with two parameters. In the first case, (1.1) has no non-trivial primitive solutions
as well. In the second case, we obtain that

                                  zr/d = Z = Pi (u, v) for some i ∈ {1, 2, . . . , k},                               (1.4)

where P1 , . . . , Pk are some polynomials in two variables u, v with integer coefficients. In some cases, Eqs. (1.4)
are easy to solve, which leads to the resolution of (1.1).
    If 1/p + 1/q + 1/d = 1, then (1.3) reduces to computing rational points on an elliptic curve. If this curve
has rank 0, then there is a finite number of such points, and they can be explicitly listed, see e.g. [77, Section
3.4.4] and [148, Section 3.21]. Then (1.3) has a finite number of primitive solutions, and it is easy to check for
which of them (if any) z = Z d/r is an integer.
    For some of the special triples in Table 1.3 more than one of the exponents p, q, r is composite, and some
exponents may have more than one non-trivial divisor d. Hence, Eq. (1.1) with special ( p, q, r) may be reducible

to several equations of the form (1.3). If any of the reduced equations has finitely many primitive solutions,
and these solutions can be computed, this immediately solves (1.1). For example, this solves equation x4 +

    In summary, below we will discuss the generalized Fermat’s Eqs. (1.1), not covered by Proposition 1.6, in
which a, b, c are positive integers with abc > 1, and exponents ( p, q, r) are either (i) prime numbers satisfying
1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1, or (ii) one of the special triples listed in Table 1.3 such that all the corresponding
Eqs. (1.3) have infinitely many primitive solutions. For each such equation, we will consider the following
problem.

Problem 1.7. For a given a, b, c, p, q, r, list all primitive integer solutions ( x, y, z) to (1.1).

    For some equations, we will also discuss the following easier problem.

Problem 1.8. For a given a, b, c, p, q, r, determine whether Eq. (1.1) has any non-trivial primitive integer
solutions.

    Because the solutions to (1.1) with xyz = 0 are easy to find, the negative answer to Problem 1.8 solves
Problem 1.7 for the corresponding equation as well. However, if (1.1) has some easy-to-find non-trivial primi-
tive integer solution, this automatically solves Problem 1.8 with a “Yes” answer, without resolving Problem 1.7
for this equation.

1.3 Some conjectures and open problems
Famous and well-believed abc conjecture of Masser and Oesterlé [113] predicts that for every real number
ǫ > 0 there exist only finitely many triples ( A, B, C ) of coprime positive integers such that

                                          A+B = C          and    C > rad( ABC )1+ǫ ,                                         (1.5)

where rad(n) denotes the product of the distinct prime factors of n. Darmon and Granville [54, Section 5.2]
observed that the abc conjecture implies the Fermat–Catalan conjecture about the finiteness of primitive integer
solutions to (1.2), and remarked that the same argument may be extended to show that it also implies the
following conjectures.

Conjecture 1.9. For any integers a, b, c such that abc 6= 0, Eq. (1.1) has only finitely many solutions6 in
integers ( x, y, z, p, q, r) such that gcd( x, y, z) = 1, p > 0, q > 0, r > 0, 1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1.

Conjecture 1.10. For any finite set S of primes, Eq. (1.1) has only finitely many solutions in integers
( a, b, c, x, y, z, p, q, r) such that gcd( ax, by, cz) = 1, p > 0, q > 0, r > 0, 1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1, and all the
prime factors of abc belong to set S.

    Conjecture 1.9 is a far-reaching generalization of Theorem 1.2 that allows p, q, r to also be variables. There
is no triple ( a, b, c) for which this conjecture has been proved. Conjecture 1.10 is even more general.
    Eq. (1.2) corresponds to the case a = b = c = 1 of Conjecture 1.9. The “next” case one may consider is
|abc| = 2. By rearranging (1.1) appropriately, we may assume that a, b, c, x, y, z are all positive integers, and
consider equation

                    ax p + byq = czr ,       gcd( x, y, z) = 1,       1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1,              abc = 2,              (1.6)

for which we are looking for solutions in positive integers a, b, c, p, q, r, x, y, z. Our computer search7 in the
range czr ≤ 280 returned only the solutions listed in Table 1.4.

    Another goal, suggested in [78, Section 3.4.2], is to describe all (not necessary primitive) integer solutions
to (1.1), or at least to (1.2). This might be easier because for some exponents ( p, q, r) there exist formulas
describing all integer solutions to these equations that give no hint on the structure of primitive solutions.
As mentioned above, such formulas have been derived in [78] for Eq. (1.1) under the condition that at least
one of the integers p, q, r is coprime with the other two. This condition fails, if, for example, ( p, q, r) =
( PQ, QR, RP) for some integers P, Q, R greater than 1. In this case, (1.1) reduces to ax PQ + byQR = czRP ,
or a + b(yR /x P ) Q = c(zR /x Q ) P . This equation can be written as

                                                  cU P − bV Q = a,                                            (1.7)

where U = zR /x Q and V = yR /x P are rational variables. If P + Q ≥ 7, this is an equation of genus g ≥ 2,
hence it has finitely many rational solutions by Falting’s Theorem [68], and, if we could list them all, we would
be able to easily describe all possible x, y, z. In the case a = b = c = 1, (1.7) reduces to U P − V Q = 1.
Mihăilescu [101], confirming famous conjecture of Catalan, proved that the only positive integer solutions to
this equation with P, Q ≥ 2 is (U, V, P, Q) = (3, 2, 2, 3), but solving this equation in rational U, V seems to
be more difficult, see [129, Theorem 12.4] and [102, Theorem 5] for a partial progress.

3 Equations of signature ( p, p, p)
3.1 Reduction to computing rational points on hyperelliptic curves
As discussed in the introduction, if the exponents ( p, q, r) in (1.1) are not special, then we may assume that all
p, q, r are prime. In this section we discuss the case p = q = r, that is, equation

                                                    ax p + by p = cz p ,                                      (3.1)

where a, b, c are positive integers, and p ≥ 5 is a prime. It is well-known [77, Section 6.2.3] and easy to check
that if ( x, y, z) with xyz 6= 0 is an integer solution to (3.1), then

                        ( X, Y ) = ( xy/z2 , a( x/z) p − c/2),      ( xz/y2 , −c(z/y) p + b/2)
                                    and   (yz/x2 , b(y/x) p + a/2)
are rational points on the curves

                     Y 2 = − abX p + c2 /4,   Y 2 = acX p + b2 /4          and Y 2 = bcX p + a2 /4,           (3.2)

respectively. In all cases, X 6= 0. These are curves of genus g = ( p − 1)/2 ≥ 2, and, by Faltings’ theo-
rem [68], each of them has a finite number of rational points. If these points can be computed for at least one of
these curves, it is straightforward to convert them into solutions to (3.1). However, computing rational points
on a high-genus curve is a difficult problem in general, and the algorithms are known only in some special
cases. One example is given by the following theorem, which is a corollary of the main result of [118], and
formulated explicitly in [77, Theorem 3.76].

                                                            14
Theorem 3.1. There is an algorithm for computing all rational solutions to any equation defining a curve of
genus g ≥ 2 whose Jacobian has rank r = 0.

    The definition of the rank of Jacobian r in Theorem 3.1 is too technical to be presented here, but we mention
that for the equation y2 = P( x), the rank r can be computed (most often estimated) using the following Magma
commands

                               > P < x >:= PolynomialRing(Rationals());
                               > C := HyperellipticCurve(P(x));
                               > J := Jacobian(C);
                               > RankBounds(J);

that can be run in the Magma calculator http://magma.maths.usyd.edu.au/calc/. In general, the output
looks like “a b”, where a and b are integers that are the lower and upper bounds for the rank r of the Jacobian.
If for at least one out of the three curves (3.2) the output is “0 0”, then its rational points can be computed by
Theorem 3.1, which in turn leads to the resolution of the corresponding Eq. (3.1).
